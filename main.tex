\begin{abstract}%
Calcium imaging records the activity of large neuronal populations in living animals, but every
recording must first be segmented into individual neurons before any activity can be read out.
Manual annotation is the bottleneck: it is slow, subjective, and does not scale. I ask whether
self-supervised learning, the paradigm behind modern language and vision foundation models, can
reduce the dependence on scarce manual labels for this task. I present
NEUROSEG,\footnote{Code: \url{https://github.com/mo-karbalaee/NEUROSEG}} an
end-to-end pipeline that segments neuronal somata in calcium-imaging recordings with a
Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) pretrained on unlabeled video and fine-tuned for
segmentation. I evaluate three hypotheses on the Neurofinder benchmark and on
cross-species (Drosophila and zebrafish) recordings: whether self-supervision helps when labels
are scarce (H1), whether representations transfer across species (H2), and whether the learned
features are temporally stable and distinct per neuron (H3). The results are a first attempt at
a novel approach rather than a decisive win. Self-supervised pretraining does not convincingly beat
a from-scratch baseline: the difference changes sign across label fractions and is in fact worst in
the scarce-label regime where the hypothesis predicted its largest gain. Cross-species representations transfer to
mouse with no practically meaningful penalty relative to same-species pretraining, and perform on par
with training from scratch. The self-supervised encoder learns to separate neurons in time
without labels, though a supervised encoder separates them best. I trace the absence of a decisive win to a
self-supervised objective that under-converges, and I lay out the concrete levers that I believe
will let the approach outperform the baselines.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Two-photon calcium imaging is one of the most widely used windows into the working brain. A
fluorescent calcium indicator brightens when a neuron fires, so a microscope movie of the tissue
is, in effect, a direct optical readout of the activity of hundreds to thousands of neurons at once,
in a living and behaving animal \citep{grienberger2012calcium,chen2013gcamp6}. The raw movie,
however, is not yet science. Before any neuron's activity can be analyzed, each neuron's cell body
(its \emph{soma}) must be located and separated from its neighbours and from the background: the
recording must be \emph{segmented}. Segmentation is therefore the first and unavoidable step of
essentially every calcium-imaging analysis pipeline.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/activity-traces.png}

\emph{Right:} the $\Delta F/F_0$ activity trace read out from each once it is segmented, the quantity
downstream analyses depend on. Data: Neurofinder 00.00, mouse cortex
\citep{peron2015barrel,neurofinder}.}
\label{fig:traces}
\end{figure}

This step is done either manually, which is slow, subjective, and does not scale, or automatically.
Automated methods already exist, from classical toolboxes to deep-learning segmenters
\citep{pachitariu2017suite2p,giovannucci2019caiman,stringer2021cellpose}, and this work adds one more.
What motivates my particular choice of method is a practical asymmetry: the strongest automated
segmenters are trained on hand-annotated data, and labeled calcium-imaging data is scarce and
expensive, whereas unlabeled calcium video is abundant: every recording session produces it as a
byproduct, with no annotation effort.

This asymmetry is precisely the setting in which self-supervised learning (SSL) has transformed
other fields. Large language models learn language by predicting held-out words in unlabeled text;
modern vision models learn image structure by predicting held-out regions of unlabeled images. The
central question of this project is whether the same idea can be adapted to calcium imaging: can a
model learn useful structure from raw, unlabeled recordings, so that only a small amount of labeled
data is then needed to segment neurons?

I investigate this question with a Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA)
\citep{lecun2022path,assran2023ijepa,bardes2024vjepa}. Unlike pixel-reconstruction approaches, a
JEPA predicts the future \emph{representation} of a frame from the present representation, in a
learned latent space rather than in pixel space. This forces the model to abstract away photometric
noise, which is severe in calcium imaging, and to focus on the structure that persists over time,
which is exactly what a neuron is. I build such a model and evaluate it as one automatic approach to
neuron segmentation.

Concretely, I ask three questions. \textbf{(H1)} Does self-supervised pretraining improve
segmentation when labeled data is scarce? \textbf{(H2)} Do representations learned on one species
transfer to another? \textbf{(H3)} Are the learned per-neuron features stable over time and distinct
between neurons? Each is tested against explicit baselines and with statistical tests.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This report makes the following contributions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{An end-to-end segmentation pipeline.} A single system with two modes, training and
    inference, and swappable segmentation stages, so a raw recording is carried all the way to
    per-neuron activity traces using either my JEPA model or a strong pretrained baseline.
  \item \textbf{A JEPA model for calcium video.} A convolutional context encoder and an EMA target
    encoder are pretrained by predicting future latents under variance-covariance regularization,
    then a light head is fine-tuned for per-pixel segmentation (Section~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{Evaluation of three hypotheses.} I test sample efficiency (H1), cross-species
    transfer (H2), and temporal representation stability (H3), each against explicit baselines and
    with statistical tests (Sections~\ref{sec:protocol}--\ref{sec:results}).
  \item \textbf{Diagnosis of the failure modes.} I identify where the approach does not yet win, trace
    the cause to an under-converged self-supervised objective, and specify the levers I expect to
    close the gap (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Neuron segmentation in calcium imaging.} Classical toolboxes such as Suite2p
\citep{pachitariu2017suite2p} and CaImAn \citep{giovannucci2019caiman} detect neurons through matrix
factorization and correlation-based region growing on the activity signal. Learned approaches began
with convolutional networks trained on labeled footprints \citep{apthorpe2016automatic}. Cellpose
\citep{stringer2021cellpose} is a generalist, pretrained deep-learning cell segmenter that
generalizes across imaging modalities without task-specific training; I use it both as the default,
out-of-the-box segmenter shipped with my pipeline and as my baseline. The Neurofinder challenge
\citep{neurofinder} is the standard public benchmark for this task.

\paragraph{Self-supervised and joint-embedding predictive learning.} SSL learns representations from
unlabeled data by solving a pretext task. Joint-embedding methods align representations of related
views while avoiding the trivial collapse to a constant. BYOL \citep{grill2020byol} showed that an
exponential-moving-average (EMA) target encoder with a stop-gradient prevents collapse without
negative pairs; VICReg \citep{bardes2022vicreg} prevents it explicitly with variance and covariance
regularization. JEPA \citep{lecun2022path} generalizes these ideas to \emph{prediction} in latent
space: I-JEPA predicts masked image regions in representation space \citep{assran2023ijepa}, and
V-JEPA extends this to video by predicting future or masked spatio-temporal regions
\citep{bardes2024vjepa}. NEUROSEG adopts the JEPA principle (context encoder, EMA target encoder,
latent-space predictor) and the VICReg regularizer, and specializes the pretext task to temporal
prediction on calcium video.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Labeled benchmark: Neurofinder (mouse).} My labeled benchmark is Neurofinder
\citep{neurofinder}, 28 movies from four laboratories (19 train / 9 test), spanning diverse brain
regions, microscopes, indicators, and signal-to-noise regimes; the flagship series is mouse barrel
cortex from the Svoboda lab \citep{peron2015barrel}. This diversity is a strength for
generalization. It also carries a limitation that shapes every design decision below: Neurofinder
labels are \emph{static per-recording footprints}, one fixed set of neuron regions per movie, not
per-frame masks. A single frame therefore hides the inactive neurons, and label definitions differ
by lab (hand-annotated somata in some series, an anatomical structural marker in others). Because
the footprint is fixed while activity flickers, the correct target for a per-frame segmenter is the
time-aggregated footprint, which motivates the temporal pooling in my model
(Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\paragraph{Unlabeled cross-species data.} For self-supervised pretraining I additionally use
unlabeled Drosophila-larva and zebrafish calcium video. No labels are needed for pretraining, so this
data is directly usable, and its two non-mouse species are what make the cross-species question (H2)
possible. This pool comprises roughly $43{,}000$ frames across $20$ cross-species
recordings, and is the unlabeled data the self-supervised pretraining draws on.

\paragraph{Preprocessing.} Each recording is intensity-normalized and resized to a common
$128\times128$ resolution, then divided into short fixed-length temporal clips. Self-supervised
pretraining additionally applies temporally consistent augmentation (a shared random crop, flip, and
rotation across a clip, plus brightness jitter and additive noise); the supervised fine-tuning branch
is not augmented, so the two H1 arms differ only in initialization.

\subsection{Model Architecture}
\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/pipeline.png}
\caption{The NEUROSEG pipeline. The training lane pretrains a JEPA encoder on unlabeled video and
fine-tunes a segmentation head; the inference lane loads a recording, preprocesses it, segments it
with either the JEPA model or the Cellpose default, extracts $\Delta F/F_0$ activity traces, and
visualizes the result.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

NEUROSEG follows the JEPA design principle of three components: a context encoder, a target encoder,
and a predictor operating in latent space, plus a regularizer that prevents representation collapse
(Figure~\ref{fig:jepa}). The model never reconstructs pixels as its main objective; it predicts
representations.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/jepa.png}
\caption{The JEPA training scheme. A context branch encodes earlier frames and predicts the latent
of a later frame; a target branch encodes that later frame with an EMA target encoder. The two are
matched in latent space under variance-covariance regularization. For segmentation, the pretrained
context encoder is reused with a light head.}
\label{fig:jepa}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Encoder.} The context encoder is a five-layer residual network \citep{he2016resnet} with
all strides set to one and no pooling, so its output feature map keeps the full input resolution.
This is deliberate: dense segmentation needs spatially aligned features. Each frame of a clip is
encoded into a latent feature map with $32$ channels per location. I raised this latent width from an
initial $8$ to $32$ after observing that too few channels could not keep neurons distinct in the H3
analysis.

\paragraph{EMA target encoder.} A second encoder of identical architecture provides the prediction
targets. It is not trained by gradients; it is updated as an exponential moving average of the
context encoder ($m=0.996$) \citep{grill2020byol}. The predictor is trained to match the
\emph{stop-gradient} output of this target encoder. This is the single most important design fix in
the project: without it, an earlier version drove the prediction loss to zero by collapsing to
trivially predictable features while learning nothing useful (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Predictor.} The predictor is a residual U-Net \citep{ronneberger2015unet}: from the
latent states of consecutive frames it predicts the latent state of the next frame, and training
minimizes the mean-squared error between this prediction and the target encoder's latent. Prediction
is thus \emph{temporal}: predict the future frame's representation, not a masked spatial patch.

\paragraph{Regularizer (VICReg).} Collapse is prevented explicitly by a variance-covariance loss
\citep{bardes2022vicreg} applied in a projected latent space: a hinge variance term keeps every
embedding dimension above unit standard deviation, and a covariance term decorrelates dimensions.
The total pretraining loss is the sum of the prediction MSE and this regularizer; a light pixel
reconstruction term additionally grounds the encoder.

\paragraph{Segmentation head and traces.} For segmentation, the encoder features are averaged over
time (collapsing the clip to one summary feature map, which matches the static footprint label) and
passed to a small two-layer convolutional head that outputs a per-pixel probability map. Thresholding
this map yields a binary segmentation, and connected foreground regions yield individual neuron
instances. During inference each neuron's activity is then read out as its $\Delta F / F_0$ trace,
with the baseline fluorescence $F_0$ estimated as the tenth percentile of the neuron's signal over
time (a robust resting level). Fine-tuning updates both the encoder and the head (true fine-tuning,
not a frozen linear probe).

\paragraph{Per-neuron embeddings (H3).} H3 measures whether an encoder represents each neuron
consistently, so it needs one embedding vector per neuron per frame. The way I extract this embedding
is a generic operation on any encoder of this architecture, and that is exactly what makes H3 a fair
comparison: I apply the \emph{identical} steps to encoders that share the architecture and differ only
in how their weights were trained. The two regimes are \emph{self-supervised} (weights from JEPA
pretraining on unlabeled video) and \emph{supervised} (the same network trained end-to-end on the
segmentation labels); the self-supervised encoder is evaluated in both its same-species and
cross-species forms. In every case the encoder preserves spatial
resolution, so its output for a frame is a dense feature map with a $32$-dimensional feature vector at
every location. For a given neuron I take its \emph{footprint}, the set of image locations the label
mask assigns to that neuron, and average the feature vectors over those locations, giving one
$32$-dimensional embedding for that neuron in that frame (masked average pooling,
Figure~\ref{fig:h3emb-pool}). Repeating this for every neuron in every frame gives each neuron a
trajectory of embeddings over time. I then compare embeddings by cosine similarity in two ways,
\emph{within-neuron} (same neuron, different frames) and \emph{between-neuron} (different neurons,
same frame), and report the gap (within minus between) separately for each encoder
(Figure~\ref{fig:h3emb-compare}). No labels enter the similarity itself; the
footprints only say which locations belong to which neuron.

\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Metrics.} Segmentation quality is measured with two complementary metrics. The
\textbf{Dice} coefficient (equivalently, pixel-level F1) measures pixel overlap,
$\mathrm{Dice}=2|P\cap G|/(|P|+|G|)$, where $P$ and $G$ are the predicted and ground-truth foreground
pixels. Dice counts pixels and carries no notion of individual neurons. I
therefore also report \textbf{detection F1}, the instance-level harmonic mean of precision
($\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}{+}\mathrm{FP})$) and recall ($\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}{+}\mathrm{FN})$) over
neuron instances, where a predicted component counts as a true positive only if it matches a
ground-truth neuron at $\mathrm{IoU}\ge 0.5$. Dice and detection F1 are not redundant: a model can
cover the right pixels (high Dice) yet merge adjacent somata into one blob (low detection F1). H3 uses
a different family entirely: the cosine similarity of pooled per-neuron embeddings, defined in
Section~\ref{sec:method}.

\paragraph{Two evaluation protocols.} I report segmentation at two granularities, and they are not
interchangeable. The \emph{per-clip} protocol scores each held-out clip at the training resolution and
averages over clips; it is used for the fraction curves and the paired tests
(Tables~\ref{tab:h1},~\ref{tab:h2}). The \emph{field-level} protocol aggregates a model's predictions
across the recording into a single native-resolution mask of the whole field of view and scores it
against the full ground-truth footprint; this is where detection F1 and the Cellpose comparison are
computed, because Cellpose segments one native-resolution image. Field-level Dice is the lower of the
two, as it is scored at full resolution over the entire footprint, including neurons that are never
active in the movie.

\paragraph{Baselines.} For H1 and H2 I compare three initializations under identical fine-tuning:
from-scratch (random initialization), same-species self-supervision (pretrained on mouse), and
cross-species self-supervision (pretrained on Drosophila and zebrafish). Cellpose, a generalist
pretrained segmenter applied zero-shot, serves as an additional external reference.

\paragraph{Statistical tests.} Because a single scalar per run hides variability, I test the
per-clip score distributions. For H1 and H2 the same held-out clips are scored by two models, so I
use the \textbf{paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test} \citep{wilcoxon1945}, which is non-parametric and
robust to the non-normal, bounded Dice distribution. For H3 the within- and between-neuron similarity
pools are unpaired, so I use the \textbf{Mann-Whitney $U$ test} \citep{mannwhitney1947} for
significance, \textbf{Cohen's $d$} \citep{cohen1988} for effect size, and a \textbf{bootstrap}
\citep{efron1979} 95\% confidence interval for the gap.

These tests carry an important caveat. The per-clip scores are not independent draws: they all come
from one recording, one split, and one seed, so the effective sample size for the scientific question
(``does self-supervision help?'') is one, not $120$. The paired tests therefore only ask whether two
already-fitted models differ on these correlated clips, which is why even a $0.017$ Dice difference
reaches $p<10^{-19}$. I report the $p$-values as descriptive of the fitted models and lead with effect
size; I do not read them as inference that self-supervision helps in general. Establishing that would
need several seeds and several recordings, with clip scores nested inside recording
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Splits and logging.} Unless stated otherwise, all experiments use one labeled recording,
Neurofinder 00.00 (mouse cortex, $330$ annotated neurons), split 80/20 into train and test with a
fixed seed. This yields about $600$ temporal clips: $120$ held out for testing and used only for final
scoring, and $\sim 480$ for training, of which the labeled fractions $\{10, 50, 75, 100\}\%$ use
roughly $48$, $240$, $360$, and $480$ clips. The binarization threshold is fixed at $0.5$ and never
tuned, and a validation split disjoint from the test clips monitors training, so no reported score is
selected on the test set. As a deliberate noise estimate, the from-scratch baseline was trained
independently for H1 and H2 (Tables~\ref{tab:h1},~\ref{tab:h2}); the two runs differ by up to $0.011$
Dice, which bounds the run-to-run variation of this setup. Differences smaller than about $0.02$ Dice
should therefore be read as within noise. Every run is logged; the complete log is in the repository.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All numbers below are the held-out test scores of the latest experiment runs recorded in the
experiment log; they are quoted verbatim, including where the self-supervised model does not win.

\subsection{H1: Does self-supervision help when labels are scarce?}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule

\midrule
10\%  & 0.626 & \textbf{0.703} \\
50\%  & 0.839 & \textbf{0.860} \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{H1 on Neurofinder mouse (held-out test Dice). Self-supervised fine-tuning and a
from-scratch baseline of identical architecture perform comparably at every labeled fraction; the

\label{tab:h1}
\end{table}

The hypothesis is that self-supervised pretraining helps most when labels are scarce.
Table~\ref{tab:h1} and Figure~\ref{fig:h1} show the opposite of that prediction. The per-clip Dice
difference between the pretrained and from-scratch models is statistically significant at both
fractions tested (paired Wilcoxon, $n=120$ clips, $p<10^{-19}$), but it changes sign: the pretrained
model is ahead by only $0.017$ at 100\% labels yet behind by $0.077$ at 10\%. Self-supervision is thus
worst exactly where it was expected to help most, so there is no robust sample-efficiency advantage
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Both learned models do clearly beat the zero-shot Cellpose reference at
the field level (Dice $0.70$--$0.71$ vs.\ $0.279$; detection F1 $0.26$--$0.28$ vs.\ $0.170$), but that
comparison is not fair to Cellpose, which never sees the target recording while my models are trained
on it.

\subsection{H2: Does self-supervision transfer across species?}

\label{tab:h2}

The hypothesis is that representations learned on one species transfer to another. The setup is
leakage-free by construction: the encoder is pretrained on species the mouse test set never contains.
Table~\ref{tab:h2} and Figure~\ref{fig:h2} show that the cross-species encoder fine-tunes to mouse
essentially on par with same-species pretraining: the per-clip penalty is only $0.011$ Dice, and while
a paired Wilcoxon test flags it as statistically significant ($n=120$, $p<10^{-20}$), the magnitude is
negligible. Against the from-scratch baseline the picture is mixed and small: from-scratch is slightly
ahead at 10\%, 50\%, and 75\%, and behind only at 100\%, and the paired difference at 100\% is
$+0.006$ Dice in favour of cross-species pretraining. All of these gaps are within the run-to-run
noise floor discussed below, so the fair reading is that cross-species pretraining is on par with, not
better than, training from scratch. Representations therefore transfer across species at a cost too
small to matter, but they do not beat a model trained directly on the target. One caveat is
interpretive: calcium activity patterns look broadly similar across species, so this transfer may
reflect the similarity of the data as much as a transferable, segmentation-specific representation.

\subsection{H3: Are the learned features stable and distinct per neuron?}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Encoder & Within-neuron & Between-neuron & Gap (within $-$ between) \\
\midrule
Cross-species SSL       & 0.968 & 0.940 & 0.028 \\
Same-species SSL        & 0.875 & 0.818 & 0.057 \\
Supervised              & 0.793 & 0.585 & \textbf{0.208} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{H3 temporal representation stability. Mean cosine similarity of pooled per-neuron
embeddings: the same neuron across frames (within) versus different neurons in the same frame
(between). A larger gap means the encoder tells neurons apart. Both self-supervised encoders show a
clear positive gap; the supervised encoder is strongest.}
\label{tab:h3}
\end{table}

Unlike H1 and H2, H3 is a diagnostic of the learned representation rather than a segmentation task: it
asks what the encoder encodes about neuron identity, not how well the full pipeline segments. The
hypothesis is that a good encoder keeps a neuron looking like itself over time while distinct
from other neurons. Using the per-neuron embeddings of Figure~\ref{fig:h3emb-pool} (no labels enter
the metric itself), Table~\ref{tab:h3} and Figure~\ref{fig:h3} show a clear, positive gap for the
self-supervised encoder (same-species gap $0.057$, cross-species $0.028$): self-supervision learned to
distinguish neurons without ever seeing a segmentation label. The supervised encoder has the largest
gap ($0.208$). A Mann-Whitney test confirms that within-neuron similarity is significantly greater than
between-neuron for every encoder (all $p \approx 0$), but the effect size is what separates them:
medium for the self-supervised encoder (Cohen's $d = 0.66$) and large for the supervised encoder
($d = 1.45$).

One caveat tempers this: the supervised encoder was trained end-to-end on the very footprint masks that
define the pooling regions here, so maximizing within-footprint consistency and between-footprint
contrast is close to what its training objective already asks for. Its $d = 1.45$ is therefore best
read as a near-tautological upper reference, not a fair competitor; the informative result is that the
self-supervised encoder, whose objective never sees these masks, still produces a clear positive gap.

\begin{figure}[t]

At the field level (Table~\ref{tab:field}), my trained models beat the zero-shot Cellpose reference on
both pixel overlap (Dice $0.70$--$0.71$ vs.\ $0.279$) and instance detection (F1 $0.26$--$0.28$ vs.\
$0.170$). Two caveats apply to this comparison. Cellpose was run with automatic cell-diameter
estimation, and its Dice of $0.279$ is low for a generalist cell segmenter, so it may simply be
misconfigured for these somata rather than genuinely weak; as it is the only external anchor,
``beats Cellpose'' should be read with caution. Separately, the
two masks are built differently (my models average their per-clip probability maps into one
field-level mask; Cellpose segments a single temporal max-projection), even though both are scored
against the same footprint.

More important than the comparison is that \emph{every} detection F1 is low: the segmenter covers the
right pixels yet merges adjacent somata into single blobs, recovering only about $155$ distinct
instances of the $330$ neurons present. This is not a minor metric gap. The stated purpose of the
pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:intro}) is to produce a per-neuron $\Delta F/F_0$ trace, and the trace of a
merged blob is the \emph{sum} of two neurons' signals, a corrupted readout rather than a slightly
worse one. Recovering fewer than half the neurons with merging therefore undermines the deliverable,
not just a score. The merging explanation, though consistent with the visible
masks, is asserted rather than demonstrated here: a distance-transform watershed or peak-splitting
ablation on the probability maps would confirm merging or, alternatively, reveal that recall is
instead limited by neurons that are never active in the movie. That diagnostic is left for future work
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

Figure~\ref{fig:curves} shows how the error behaves epoch to epoch. During pretraining the training

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why there is no decisive win, and why I am not discouraged.} The most informative result
of this project was an early \emph{negative} one: a first H1 run had self-supervised pretraining
\emph{losing} to the baseline at every fraction. Diagnosing it was the turning point. The prediction
loss had collapsed to near zero while segmentation stayed poor, the signature of a degenerate
self-supervised solution: features that are easy to predict but carry no information. The root causes
were concrete. There was no stop-gradient target, so the encoder could satisfy the predictor by
collapsing; the pixel-grounding signal never reached the encoder; the fine-tuning learning rates were
confounded between the two arms; and, decisively, pretraining and fine-tuning used the \emph{same
single recording}, so the self-supervised model had no extra information to exploit. I fixed the
first three (an EMA target encoder with stop-gradient, a reconstruction term that trains the encoder,
and a configurable, matched fine-tuning learning rate), which is why the current H1 is a tie rather
than a loss. The fourth is not yet fixed at scale, and it is the reason a tie is the ceiling of the
present setup: with equal data, self-supervision has nothing to win with. That the approach already
matches a strong baseline, transfers across species at no cost, and induces per-neuron temporal
structure without labels, all while the self-supervised objective still under-converges, is what
makes me believe the ceiling is much higher than these numbers. Concretely, a decisive H1 result
would be a positive and significant Dice gap at the 10\% fraction that holds across seeds and
recordings, rather than the sign-flipping difference I observe now.

\paragraph{An alternative I tested: is the pretext task too easy?} Before attributing the flat loss to
budget alone, I considered a competing hypothesis: because calcium video has essentially static anatomy
with only brightness fluctuating, predicting frame $t{+}1$'s latent from frame $t$'s might be solvable
by near-identity, in which case the objective would teach the encoder very little and \emph{more data
would not help}. As a partial check (the definitive test, comparing the trained predictor against an
identity predictor, needs the pretraining predictor, which the checkpoints do not store) I measured
how copyable the target actually is. On held-out clips, consecutive-frame latents from the pretrained
encoder have a mean cosine of only $0.54$ and a relative MSE near $1.0$, barely above latents from
different clips at the same location ($0.52$). The target is therefore \emph{not} copyable: an identity
predictor would not solve the task, so triviality does not explain the flat loss. If anything, that
temporal neighbours are almost as far apart as unrelated frames points back to weak, under-converged
temporal features rather than an easy task. A harder pretext task (larger temporal offsets, or masked
spatio-temporal blocks in the style of V-JEPA \citep{bardes2024vjepa}) is still worth trying, but the
more likely lever is a better-converged backbone with more data.

\paragraph{The levers I expect to close the gap.} Assuming the pretext task is non-trivial, then in
order of expected value: (i) a genuinely larger and more diverse \emph{unlabeled} pretraining pool,
the precondition H1 actually requires and the lever SSL responds to most reliably; (ii) a stronger
self-supervised backbone, with a better predictor and target encoder and a longer, better-converged
pretraining schedule; and (iii) a direct attack on residual representation collapse through tuning of
the variance-covariance coefficients and EMA momentum, and a linear-probe diagnostic to measure
representation quality independently of the fine-tuning schedule.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The main limitations are the following. \textbf{(1) Instance separation.} Detection F1 is low for all
models ($0.17$--$0.28$): the system does not yet separate touching neurons into instances. \textbf{(2) Single seed and
single recording.} The headline H1/H3 numbers come from one seed and one recording split into train
and test, so variance across seeds and recordings is not yet characterized. \textbf{(3) Under-converged
pretraining.} The self-supervised objective does not fully converge under the current schedule and
data budget, which caps any benefit it can confer. \textbf{(4) Unfair Cellpose comparison.} My models
are trained on the target recording while Cellpose is zero-shot, so ``beats Cellpose'' overstates the
result. \textbf{(5) Protocol inconsistency.} The per-clip and field-level Dice evaluations differ in
resolution and in how a single mask is formed; unifying them is a priority. \textbf{(6) Interpretation
of H2.} Cross-species transfer may partly reflect the similarity of calcium data across species rather
than a segmentation-specific representation. \textbf{(7) Untested architectural choice.} I adopt JEPA
over simpler joint-embedding methods (for example VICReg or BYOL on augmented pairs) on the argument
that latent prediction abstracts photometric noise, but I do not ablate that choice against those
baselines, so it remains a motivated preference rather than a demonstrated one. Each of these is a
known, bounded issue rather than a surprise, which is why I treat the pipeline as ready ground on which
to push further.

\subsection{Conclusion and Outlook}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This was a first attempt at a novel approach: self-supervised, JEPA-style neuron segmentation for
calcium imaging. I did not decisively beat the baselines, and I understand exactly why. The
self-supervised model already matches a strong supervised baseline, transfers across species with no
meaningful penalty, and learns to tell neurons apart in time without a single label, all while its
self-supervised objective still under-converges. I have a working pipeline and the
models and implementations to keep working on all three hypotheses. The ground work is done. There is
room for improvement and it is worth the effort: a stronger self-supervised backbone, a direct attack
on representation collapse, and much more unlabeled calcium video are, I am convinced, enough to push
this novel approach past the baselines.
